Mobility, Flexibility, Balance and Coordination — A 52-Week Contemplative Curriculum for Movement and Spiritual Vitality

Free. Peer-led. No ranks. No branding.

To God be the glory.

A year of training the body's movement capacities — mobility, flexibility, balance, coordination — as sacred stewardship, then living and revising the plan as Jesus Christ would do. Plans are always imperfect, but the whole point of planning is not the document; it is the implementing, refactoring, and revising. The POINT of a plan is a starting roadmap that gets overhauled the moment the moving starts, because new OPPORTUNITIES (and new ranges) will arise.

The MOST IMPORTANT part of this plan is gratitude discipline: the situation is always far better than one hoped for, though never exactly what one hoped for. Humans are fearfully and wonderfully made for dynamic movement, and the household itself is the gym: every load of laundry, every scrub of the floor, every sit-down and get-up is a rep. We cultivate curiosity rather than frustration with changing physical abilities, and compassionate yet challenging practice that supports lifelong independence.

Yes, these questions have that GetAfterIt energy—because your stiffness isn't just physical, it's spiritual rebellion against the body God gave you. Stop pretending immobility is inevitable. Get serious about stewardship. You aren't ready to die YET!

1. What this document is, and what it is not

Is: a full year of weekly, 75-minute sessions for a small group (or one stiff believer with a notebook) examining current movement capabilities, quality of movement patterns, flexibility development, balance integration through aging, motor control, environmental factors, recovery techniques, and the long-term vision of movement capability maintained for decades. Every question of the source framework is retained in its original voice and given its week; each week is deepened with one additional passage of Scripture and one piece of field material — chief among them Kelly and Juliet Starrett's Built to Move and The Ready State, because nobody has made a better case that the floor is free, the sit-to-stand is a vital sign, and daily life itself is the mobility program.

Is not: a credential program. No ranks, no tests you fail, no graduation. Nothing here gives one person authority over another. The person who needs a handrail today is exactly as welcome as the person who can sit and rise at a score of ten.

Is not: a gymnastics program. Most of the year's work happens inside ordinary life — sitting on the floor to watch television, squatting to scrub the bathtub, rising from the carpet without hands, carrying the groceries with a tall spine. The extraordinary positions appear only as tests and drills; the practice is the household. Anyone with pain, injury, dizziness, or a heart condition moves with their physician's clearance, and within pain-free range, always.

Every week has five parts: a focus work assignment (the week's questions, worked in the body, not just the head), a breath practice (a named, counted breathing exercise done as its own segment), one concept to carry, one deepening (a passage of Scripture plus field material), and about four sentences of teaching written out so nobody has to improvise a lesson they do not feel ready to give.

2. How to read a week's entry

  • Focus work. The question(s) from the framework, labeled base, build, peak, or recover (explained in section 5), with their verses.
  • Breath practice. A named exercise with actual counts — a class inside the class, not a warm-up and not a cooldown. Movement work lives or dies on the breath that shapes it.
  • Concept. One idea, one sentence, for the week.
  • Deepening. One additional passage of Scripture, plus one piece of field material — Starrett doctrine, movement science, or the testimony of the eighty-year-olds who never stopped getting up off the floor.
  • Teaching. Three to five sentences to read aloud or paraphrase: what the question means, why it matters to a person who is creaky or new or ashamed, and how it connects to what the breath just did.

Reading the teaching in your own words is fine. Skipping it is not — the sentences are the actual lesson, and the movement is the illustration.

3. The whole mover: four things we are actually training

A mobility program that only trains range of motion produces a person who can touch their toes and still cannot get off the floor without grunting. Each session touches four dimensions, and each week names which one it leans on.

The physical dimension is the obvious one: joints moved through full range, tissues loaded and released, balance challenged in a controlled dose, the body re-learning positions modern life deleted. Trainable in an hour, measurable in a season — the sit-and-rise score is a number anyone can track.

The mental dimension is motor control and understanding: knowing that the knee's pain is the ankle's fault, that "tight hamstrings" is often a nervous-system speed limit, that stiffness is a position history, not a destiny. A person who can name which joint is blocked has gained something no amount of random stretching gives.

The emotional dimension is what happens when the body won't do what it used to: frustration, embarrassment on the floor, the quiet grief of a lost cartwheel. Real limitation met inside guaranteed safety, where nobody is allowed to mock anybody. We never tell anyone their body-image is being healed here; we provide conditions and let time work.

The spiritual dimension is the framework's whole thesis: movement as stewardship of a fearfully-made temple, every get-up off the floor a small resurrection rehearsal. One passage, read twice, sat with in silence, answered with one sentence if a person wants to speak.

The breath practice is the hinge where all four meet: it is physical (position and breath are one system — if you can't breathe in a position, you don't own it), mental (counting requires attention), emotional (a slow exhale is the fastest down-shift known), and spiritual (God breathed, and dust became a mover).

4. The fixed shape of every session

The session is the same every week of the year. That sameness is the single most important design decision in this document. A person who drops in twice a year should recognize the shape immediately.

That is seventy-five minutes. For a solo mover, keep the timer and the segments. In the stiffest week of the year, shorten the focus work, never the verse circle.

5. The four-week rhythm inside every block

The year is thirteen blocks of four weeks. Each block has one theme, and inside each block the weeks follow the same wave — because tissue, like faith, grows by exposure, rest, and gratitude, in that order.

Week one — Base (teach). The block's opening questions, held at conversational intensity, slow movement, no fatigue, a great deal of talking. The goal is that everyone leaves able to name the week's concept and feel it in the body. If people are exhausted, the dose was too hot. If people cannot state the concept, the teaching was too short.

Week two — Build (repetition). The block's questions returned to daily — movement snacks stacked into every transition: sit to the floor during the news, couch stretch during the show, stand on one leg while the coffee brews. Volume without intensity. Correction is offered quietly and individually, never as a public demonstration of someone's error.

Week three — Peak (capacity). The block's hardest question, held under genuine but controlled load — the end-range hold, the fatigued balance, the crawl that humbles. Nobody is pushed past what they can recover from by the next morning. The floor humbles enough on its own; we do not add to it.

Week four — Recover (flow). Light, integrated movement — the block's skills used without demanding anything new — followed by a longer verse circle, usually sitting on the floor. The bring-a-friend week: arrive late, leave early, miss half, nothing is disrupted.

6. Why the breath practice sits where it does

The breath comes after the movement work and before the verse circle, on purpose. After forty-five minutes on the floor, most people are either winded or wound: attention narrowed, jaw set, nervous system guarding the very ranges it was asked to open. Eight to ten minutes of slow, spacious, nasal breathing tells the guard the inspection is over, and makes the room quiet enough that a single verse can actually be heard instead of merely recited.

This is also where the curriculum trains what most mobility culture trains badly: owning positions by staying calm in them. Most stretching produces a person who yanks at ranges while holding their breath — the nervous system reads that as threat and locks down harder. What we want instead is the Starrett test applied to every position all year: can you breathe in it? — spacious, slow, through the nose. If you can breathe there, you own there. Each breath practice has three jobs, named aloud when introduced:

  1. Down-regulate. Long exhale, slow count, no strain. The permission job — range is granted by a relaxed system, not extracted from it.
  2. Widen. Spacious inhale, ribs and belly moving — the position job, because breath and posture are one machine.
  3. Attend. One verse, one joint, one honest sentence of thanks. The stewardship job: the body was made, and the Maker is worth consulting about its maintenance.

Block 1 · Weeks 1–4 · Core Principle: Movement as Sacred Stewardship

Week 1 · The Sit-and-Rise: Your First Vital Sign

Focus work — base week. The core principle, held slowly: movement is sacred stewardship of divine design, and the year begins with an honest number. The block's anchor test, done barefoot: stand, cross one leg over the other, lower yourself to the floor cross-legged, and rise again — without using hands, knees, or a handrail if you honestly can. Start at ten points; deduct for every assist and wobble. Whatever you scored, write it down: it is this year's blood pressure. Breath practice — "The Ten-Point Breath." Sitting cross-legged on the floor (however you got there — assists are allowed this week): in for four, out for six, eight rounds. If the position steals your breath, the position is the assignment, not the count. Concept. The get-up off the floor is a vital sign: the score predicts decades. Deepening. Psalm 139:14 — "I praise you because I am fearfully and wonderfully made; your works are wonderful, I know that full well." And the material: the Sit-and-Rise test opens Kelly and Juliet Starrett's Built to Move (The Ready State) precisely because it compresses a whole movement life into one motion — hips, ankles, balance, coordination, and the courage of the descent; and the research the Starretts cite links floor-rise competence to long-term health and independence. Children do it at three without thinking; the chair is what un-teaches us. You were made to keep it. Teaching. The framework's first assignment is a number, not a workout — because you cannot steward what you refuse to measure, and because the psalm insists the machinery was wonderfully made, which makes surrender to rigor mortis a theological error, not an aging strategy. Take the test with grace and without drama: a low score is not shame, it is a starting line. The whole year is built to raise that number, and the first practice — breathing, on the floor — starts tonight.

Week 2 · The Household Is the Gym

Focus work — build week. Daily, and this is the week's real program: every ordinary chore becomes a movement test. Lunge with the vacuum; crawl to reach under furniture; scrub the bathroom while kneeling, not bending; carry the laundry basket tall, one trip, both hands; sit on the floor to fold clothes; and every time you drop something or do something that requires extra steps, consider the get-down-and-get-back-up a gift -- focus on the biomechanics. The question held all week: where did today's chores ask for range I refused to give? Breath practice — "The Chore Exhale." At each chore's start: one round, in for four while finding the working position, out for six while dropping into it. If you cannot breathe in the scrubbing position, you are not the owner of it yet. Concept. Nobody needs two hours daily; everyone needs a house that asks questions and a body that answers. Deepening. Colossians 3:23 — "Whatever you do, work at it with all your heart, as working for the Lord, not for human masters." And the material: this is the Starretts' "movement snacks" doctrine made domestic — ten squats waiting for the kettle, a 90/90 hip switch during the news, one extra trip up the stairs with the groceries; the research on habitual movement says the day's accumulated positions matter more than the hour in the gym, and the home is the only gym you visit twenty times a day anyway. The bathtub doesn't know it's a mobility tool. That's between you and it. Teaching. This week rewrites the whole idea of a training program: the reps were hiding in the chores all along, and the difference between a workout and a surrender is mostly whether you took the squat when it was offered. Notice the spiritual rhyme — the framework calls the body a temple, and temples have floors; somebody has always had to keep the floors. Work heartily, says the verse, and let the kneeling be the warm-up it always secretly was.

Week 3 · Basic Maintenance: The Starrett Doctrine

Focus work — base week. The block's field question, taken slowly: what would change if you treated yourself as a machine you are qualified to maintain — ten focused minutes a day, every day, forever? Breath practice — "The Maintenance Breath." Spacious, slow, nasal — in for four, expanding ribs and belly; out for six, jaw loose. Ten rounds, sitting tall. This is the breath the whole year will ask for in every hard position. Concept. All human beings should be able to perform basic maintenance on themselves. Deepening. Proverbs 4:7 — "The beginning of wisdom is this: Get wisdom. Though it cost all you have, get understanding." And the material: Kelly Starrett — doctor of physical therapy, CrossFit coach, founder of The Ready State and the decade-running MobilityWOD project — built his whole teaching on that maintenance thesis: that position is a skill, that "tight" is usually a nervous-system speed limit rather than short tissue, and that ten honest minutes daily at the edge of range beats the monthly heroics that end in strains. His tools are unglamorous: the couch stretch, banded joint distractions, the foam roller, time on the floor. The framework's GetAfterIt energy and the DPT's patience meet exactly here: get after it, slowly, daily, for decades. Teaching. The block's third week installs the year's engine: not a stretching routine but a maintenance mindset — you are the owner-operator of one body, and the owner's manual is learnable. Notice the doctrine's humility: it does not promise circus flexibility; it promises competence at your own edges. Take ten minutes tonight — floor, couch stretch, breath — and you have done the whole assignment.

Week 4 · Movement as Worship: The Floor Is Free

Focus work — recover week. Light integration, longer verse circle, bring-a-friend week. The block's questions in a sentence each, then the week's gift practice: tonight, sit on the floor to watch television. Cross-legged, 90/90, legs long — vary it when it gets uncomfortable; fidgeting is the practice. Thirty minutes on the floor, and notice what your hips say by the credits. Breath practice — "The Sabbath Sit." On the floor, wherever it's comfortable this minute: in for four, out for six, unhurried. Five minutes. The sitting is the practice; the breathing keeps it honest. Concept. The floor is free real estate, and worship has postures. Deepening. 1 Corinthians 6:19–20 — "Do you not know that your bodies are temples of the Holy Spirit? … Therefore honor God with your bodies." And the material: the Starretts' thirty-minutes-of-floor-sitting prescription is the most economical mobility intervention in print — the positions humans used for millennia, restored nightly, no equipment beyond gravity; and the framework's own theology completes it: kneelers, prostration, sitting cross-legged around fires — the church's prayer postures and the body's oldest resting positions were never separate lists. The couch will still be there tomorrow. So, if you keep this up, will your hips. Teaching. The block closes by fusing its two anchors: maintenance and worship — the DPT's floor and the temple's floor. Notice neither asks for your feelings; both ask for your presence, in a position, breathing. Next block: the daily questions, the small interrogations that keep the household gym honest.

Block 2 · Weeks 5–8 · Daily Contemplative Practice (Part I)

Week 5 · Morning Stiffness and the Sitting Addiction

Focus work — base week. Retained, morning questions: That morning stiffness you've accepted as "normal aging"—when exactly were you planning to fight back against this slow-motion surrender to rigor mortis? (Psalm 139:14 — "I am fearfully and wonderfully made.") And: Your hip flexors are tighter than a Pharisee's doctrine—still wondering why your back hurts or ready to admit your sitting addiction needs intervention? (Romans 12:1 — "Offer your bodies as a living sacrifice.") Breath practice — "The First Five Minutes." Before standing: on your back, knees bent, in for four, out for six, five rounds; then one slow sit-to-stand from the bed's edge. The morning's first meeting with gravity is scheduled, not stumbled into. Concept. Morning stiffness is a position history, not an age — it is the receipt for yesterday's shapes. Deepening. Lamentations 3:22–23 — "His compassions never fail. They are new every morning." And the material: Starrett's Deskbound argument in one line — the chair adapts the body to its own shape, and the hip flexors that ache at dawn spent yesterday folding; the countermeasure is not a better mattress but more positions in the day, including the floor sit and the couch stretch counter-pose. Compassions are new every morning; so is synovial fluid. Both respond to use. Teaching. The framework's first morning questions name the two favorite alibis: "normal aging" (stiffness is really position history) and the tight-hip back-pain mystery (there is no mystery — it was the sitting). Take the first-five-minutes practice daily and watch the mornings change inside two weeks. The fight-back against rigor mortis is not heroic; it is a bedtime and a floor.

Week 6 · The Temple and the Preparation

Focus work — build week. Retained: Ready to see daily movement as worship instead of treating your temple like a neglected building? (1 Corinthians 6:19–20 — "Your bodies are temples of the Holy Spirit.") And: Your movement preparation is nonexistent—respecting the temple or treating it like disposable commodity? (1 Corinthians 3:16–17 — "God's Spirit dwells in your midst.") Breath practice — "The Preparation." Two minutes before whatever the day asks first — the walk, the workout, the shoveling: circles, neck to ankles, every joint, one breath per circle. The oldest warm-up in the world, restored to daily duty. Concept. Preparation is not the optional part of movement; it is the respect part. Deepening. Ecclesiastes 10:10 — "If the ax is dull… more strength is needed, but skill will bring success." And the material: the Starretts' controlled articular rotations — CARs, the daily joint circles — are the maintenance program of the whole Ready State method: each joint moved slowly through its full circle at low intensity, daily, for the life of the joint; the dull ax is the un-warmed body, and the skill that sharpens it costs two minutes. The temple verse does the theology: buildings this well made deserve a caretaker who circles the property. Teaching. This week's questions turn preparation from a gym nicety into a temple duty — and the practice is almost embarrassingly small: two minutes of circles, every morning, before the day loads the joints. Notice the snark is aimed at the neglect, not the creakiness: nobody is mocked for a stiff morning, only for pretending the building maintains itself. Do the circles daily and log what changes.

Week 7 · Cool-Downs and Movement Snacks

Focus work — base week. Retained, evening questions: How many more times will you skip the cool-down mobility work that prevents tomorrow's stiffness? (Proverbs 20:4 — "Sluggards do not plow in season; at harvest time they look but find nothing.") And: Your movement snacks throughout the day—non-existent or strategically stacked into every transition? (Deuteronomy 6:7 — "Talk about them… when you walk along the road, when you lie down and when you get up.") Breath practice — "The Evening Plow." Two minutes of the day's best positions — couch stretch, thoracic circles, feet-up-the-wall — one long exhale each, before bed. Tonight's two minutes are tomorrow morning's harvest. Concept. Stiffness tomorrow is sown (or not) tonight. Deepening. Proverbs 6:6–8 — "Go to the ant… consider its ways and be wise! … it stores its provisions in summer." And the material: "movement snacks" is the Starretts' own coinage for exactly this — five air squats at the counter, a 90/90 switch during the commercial, calf stretches at the curb; the Deuteronomy command makes the pattern liturgical: walking, lying down, getting up — the transitions themselves are the appointed times. The ant stores in summer; the mover stretches at transitions. Teaching. The evening questions finally put numbers on the framework's quietest lie — that stiffness arrives by weather instead of by sowing. Stack snacks into transitions all week (the verse literally provides the schedule: road, bed, rising) and plow the two minutes at night. Tomorrow morning's testimony will be data.

Week 8 · The Evening Ritual

Focus work — recover week. Retained: How many more years of morning stiffness before you implement evening mobility rituals? (Psalm 63:6 — "On my bed I remember you; I think of you through the watches of the night.") The block's questions in a sentence each; longer verse circle — taken on the floor. Breath practice — "The Watches." Lying in bed, feet heavy, one breath per watch of the night ahead: in for four, out for six, ten rounds. The last mobility work of the day is remembering. Concept. The last position of the day is a rehearsal for the first one. Deepening. Psalm 4:8 — "In peace I will lie down and sleep, for you alone, Lord, make me dwell in safety." And the material: the Starretts' floor-sitting and pre-sleep mobility prescriptions converge with the psalmist's bedtime practice — the body that ends the day in cared-for positions reports less morning stiffness within a week or two of consistency; the psalm adds the deeper layer: the resting body is held, not merely parked. Evening ritual is stewardship wearing pajamas. Teaching. The block closes by making the evening ritual permanent and liturgical: the bed is a place where a remembering body lies down, and a two-minute plow plus ten slow breaths completes the day's maintenance the way Compline completes the day's prayers. Do it nightly this week, and next block — the weekly cycle — will find you already moving.

Block 3 · Weeks 9–12 · Weekly Movement Cycles (Part II)

Week 9 · Monday: Gravity Is Winning by Default

Focus work — base week. Retained: How many more years will you let gravity win without putting up a fight through daily mobility work? (Isaiah 40:31 — "Those who hope in the Lord will renew their strength.") That balance you're losing incrementally—still calling it "getting older" or ready to admit it's neglect of God's temple disguised as inevitability? (1 Corinthians 6:19–20.) Your coordination is deteriorating faster than your excuses are multiplying—when will you stop talking and start moving? (James 1:22 — "Do what it says.") That movement assessment you're avoiding—afraid of the truth or ready to establish baselines? (Lamentations 3:40 — "Let us examine our ways and test them.") Breath practice — "Monday's Baseline." One minute on each leg, eyes soft, near a counter: in for four, out for six. Wobbly is data. Write the data down. Concept. Gravity is not your enemy; it is your unpaid trainer — the only question is whether you attend the sessions. Deepening. Psalm 119:59–60 — "I have considered my ways and have turned my steps to your statutes. I will hasten, without delay." And the material: balance and coordination decline silently for years before the first fall announces it — the research the fall-prevention world cites is blunt: the systems that save you at the ice patch are the ones you trained at the countertop decades earlier. Monday's assessment is the framework's mercy: examine and test, before the environment does it for you on black ice. Teaching. Monday's four questions all strip the same alibi — "aging" doing the work that neglect did — and replace it with the only antidote: baselines. Take the single-leg minute and the sit-and-rise score, write both down, and you have established what a whole year of quiet work can grow. The hastening psalm is right: without delay, because gravity never takes a day off.

Week 10 · Tuesday: Flexibility and the Frozen Pillar

Focus work — build week. Retained: Still treating flexibility like it's optional—who exactly are you trying to fool? (Proverbs 31:17 — "Her arms are strong for her tasks.") Your hamstrings are so tight you can't touch your toes—but sure, keep pretending flexibility doesn't matter for daily function! (Philippians 3:13 — "Forgetting what is behind and straining toward what is ahead.") That thoracic spine is frozen like Lot's wife but you wonder why your shoulders hurt—ready to mobilize or keep looking back? (Genesis 19:26.) How long will you confuse feeling tight with actually being inflexible—addressing root causes or just sensations? (Proverbs 20:5 — "The purposes of a person's heart are deep waters.") Breath practice — "The Couch Stretch Count." The week's workhorse: couch stretch, two minutes a side, breathing spacious and nasal — if you must hold your breath, you've found your true range, and the breath is the ruler. Concept. Feeling tight is a nervous-system opinion; being inflexible is a position fact — and only one of them answers to daily practice. Deepening. Philippians 3:13–14, completed — "Forgetting what is behind and straining toward what is ahead, I press on toward the goal." And the material: the Starrett doctrine again — much of "tight" is protective tone, a speed limit the nervous system sets and raises as it learns the position is safe, which is why breath and time at the edge outperform yanking; and the thoracic spine is the shoulder's chassis — the frozen mid-back hands its job to the shoulder, which files the complaint. Lot's wife is the framework's best anatomy joke: a pillar that only looks back is a pillar that can't reach forward either. Teaching. Tuesday is range day, and its four questions converge on one skill: telling the sensation from the fact, then treating the fact with breath and minutes. The couch stretch — the Starretts' signature hip-flexor antidote to the chair — is this week's household prescription, done during television, without heroics. Straining toward what is ahead, it turns out, is a mid-back verse as much as a life verse.

Week 11 · Wednesday Balance, Thursday Coordination

Focus work — peak week. Retained, Wednesday's: Your proprioception is shot but you're still pretending those stumbles are "just not paying attention"—ready to train or ready to fall? (Proverbs 4:26 — "Give careful thought to the paths for your feet.") Still treating balance training like it's for "old people" while your stability deteriorates daily—pride or stupidity? (Proverbs 16:18 — "Pride goes before… a fall.") That vestibular system you never train—waiting for vertigo or working on balance preemptively? (Proverbs 22:3.) Still thinking balance is about standing still instead of dynamic stability—missing the point much? (Psalm 16:8 — "With him at my right hand, I will not be shaken.") And Thursday's: That coordination you need for emergency response—building it daily or hoping you'll magically have it when crisis hits? (2 Timothy 4:2 — "Be prepared in season and out of season.") Your coordination under fatigue is non-existent—training it or hoping you'll never need it when tired? (Isaiah 40:29.) Your movement quality looks like a rusty robot—still thinking speed matters more than control? (Ecclesiastes 9:11 — "The race is not to the swift.") That movement complexity you avoid—brain challenging or brain dead training? (Romans 12:2 — "Be transformed by the renewing of your mind.") Breath practice — "The Sensory Sweep." Single-leg stands with eyes soft, then eyes closed, near a counter: one breath cycle per attempt, wobbles counted honestly. The proprioceptive system is trained by being asked questions, daily, gently. Concept. Balance is a skill that fails silently; coordination under fatigue is a plan that fails loudly — both are trained or lost. Deepening. Psalm 17:5 — "My steps have held to your paths; my feet have not slipped." And the material: the fall-prevention science is emphatic — balance is among the most trainable capacities at any age, and the cheap drills (single-leg stands, heel-to-toe lines, head-turns while walking, eyes-closed work near support) pay directly into the ice-patch reflexes; and coordination is cognition wearing shoes — novel movement patterns (crawling, cross-crawls, non-dominant-hand chores) are the original brain training. The framework's snark about "balance is for old people" inverts the fact: training it is how you avoid becoming the person the stereotype describes. Teaching. The peak week holds two whole days at once because they are one capacity in two costumes: staying upright, and staying orderly — and both are quiet until the day they aren't. Train the drills daily at the counter and the curb; take one novel pattern (the crawl you haven't done since infancy is next month's headline) and be humbled on purpose. The race is not to the swift; it is to the person still standing in it.

(Ezekiel 37:5 — "I will make breath enter you, and you will come to life.") Your breathing is shallow and disconnected from movement—still wondering why you gas out quickly? (Genesis 2:7.) That rotational mobility you need for life—training it or living in a sagittal plane prison? (Ezekiel 37:7 — "…the bones came together.") Your breath doesn't match your movement—disconnected systems or integrated whole? (Job 33:4.) Saturday's: That neural tension limiting your flexibility—addressing it with nerve glides or just yanking on tight muscles? (Psalm 139:13 — "You knit me together.") How many chiropractor visits before you realize YOU need to mobilize daily, not just get adjusted? (Matthew 9:12.) The week closes where it will close for fifty more weeks: on the floor, getting up. Concept. The week's back half is plumbing and poetry: hydrate the fascia, glide the nerves, wake the sleeping areas — and notice that the stiff body and the guarded heart are one body. Deepening. Ezekiel 37:10 — "Breath entered them; they came to life and stood up on their feet." And the material: the valley of dry bones is the framework's own fascia chapter — tissue needs water, slow pressure, and movement to un-stick, and nerve glides (gentle, never yanked) address the tingling tightness that stretching can't; and the Starretts' Sunday-tier reminder: the developmental positions — squat, kneel, sit, rise — are the body's native alphabet, and children drill them daily without knowing.
