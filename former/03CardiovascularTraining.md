# Cardiovascular Training — A 52-Week Contemplative Curriculum for Heart and Soul

*Free. Peer-led. No ranks. No branding.*

**To God be the glory.**

***A year of training the heart — the physical one and the spiritual one — then living and revising the plan as Jesus Christ would do.*** Plans are always imperfect, but the whole point of planning is not the document; it is the implementing, refactoring, and revising. The POINT of a plan is a starting roadmap that gets overhauled the moment *the training* starts, because new OPPORTUNITIES (and new PRs) will arise.

The MOST IMPORTANT part of this plan is **gratitude discipline**: the situation is always far better than one hoped for, though never exactly what one hoped for. Cardiovascular training is reframed here from obligation to privilege and celebration of continuing capability. The heart is the first organ to start working and the last to rest, it beats about a hundred thousand times a day without asking for applause, and it is yours to steward.

*Yes, you will detect that these questions have that GetAfterIt AllSixDaysOfTheWeekLong Monday morning energy that characterizes Ancient Guy Fitness... get going... you aren't ready to die YET!*

## 1. What this document is, and what it is not

**Is:** a full year of weekly, 75-minute sessions for a small group (or one athlete with a notebook) exploring the evolving relationship with cardio exercise across life stages — heart rate monitoring and training zones, fitness assessment, effective progression, integration with sleep, nutrition, and recovery, equipment and technology choices, motivation, and a long-term vision for cardiovascular longevity. Every question of the source framework is retained in its original voice and given its week; each week is deepened with one additional passage of Scripture and one piece of training material — including the Norwegian cross-country tradition and Alex Karp's testimony about what it does for the brain, which is the most oxygen-hungry organ you own.

**Is not:** a credential program. No ranks, no tests, no graduation. Nothing here gives one person authority over another. The person who walked their first mile this morning is exactly as welcome as the person who skied forty kilometers.

**Is not:** a medical program. The group's leader is not a cardiologist, and this document does not replace one. Anyone with symptoms, risk factors, or a device in their chest trains with their physician's clearance or not at all. Every week's intensity is scaled to whoever is present.

Every week has five parts: a **focus work** assignment (the week's questions, wrestled with on the move), a **breath practice** (a named, counted breathing exercise done as its own segment), one **concept** to carry, one **deepening** (a passage of Scripture plus a piece of training material that opens the week's question further), and about four sentences of **teaching** written out so nobody has to improvise a lesson they do not feel ready to give.

## 2. How to read a week's entry

- **Focus work.** The question(s) from the framework, labeled *base*, *build*, *peak*, or *recover* (explained in section 5), with their verses.
- **Breath practice.** A named exercise with actual counts — a class inside the class, not a warm-up and not a cooldown.
- **Concept.** One idea, one sentence, for the week.
- **Deepening.** One additional passage of Scripture, plus one piece of training material — the Norwegian method, exercise science, or the testimony of people who have walked (or skied) this road for decades.
- **Teaching.** Three to five sentences to read aloud or paraphrase: what the question means, why it matters to a person who is tired or new or sore, and how it connects to what the breath just did.

Reading the teaching in your own words is fine. Skipping it is *not* — the sentences are the actual lesson, and the workout is the illustration.

## 3. The whole athlete: four things we are actually training

A cardio program that only trains the engine produces a person who is fitter and still oblivious. Each session touches four dimensions, and each week names which one it leans on.

**The physical dimension** is the obvious one: the heart, the vessels, the mitochondria, the breath. Trainable in an hour, measurable in a season — resting heart rate drops, recovery quickens, stairs stop being an Olympic event.

**The mental dimension** is knowledge wielded: knowing your zones and not just quoting them, reading your own data like a shepherd reads his flocks, telling discomfort from danger. A person who can state what Zone 2 is *and why they were in it for two hours* has gained something no amount of suffering alone gives.

**The emotional dimension** is what happens under real stress — the interval that stares back, the alarm clock negotiation, the eighth week of no visible progress. These are small stresses, but real ones, arriving in a place where nobody is allowed to mock anybody. Real stress plus guaranteed safety is most of what this program is for.

**The spiritual dimension** is the heart the Bible actually talks about — the seat of will and worship, which God searches and offers to renew. One passage, read twice, sat with in silence, answered with one sentence if a person wants to speak. The verse is the teacher; the leader is the person who keeps the room quiet long enough for it to be heard.

The **breath practice** is the hinge where all four meet: it is physical (breathing is the engine's throttle), mental (counting requires attention), emotional (a long exhale is the fastest known way to down-shift the nervous system), and spiritual (every Scripture about breath assumes a God who breathed life into dust).

## 4. The fixed shape of every session

The session is the same every week of the year. That sameness is the single most important design decision in this document. A person who drops in twice a year should recognize the shape immediately.

| Minutes | Segment | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | Arrival | Shoes, hydration, weather check. Anyone new gets the one-page zone card (week 10's handout). |
| 5–10 | Circle | Welcome. The week's questions, read aloud, with their verses. One honest answer from whoever leads. |
| 10–22 | Preparation | Joint and movement preparation, in the same order every week, plus whatever the day's focus work requires. |
| 22–45 | Main focus work | Base, build, peak, or recover, depending on the week. |
| 45–52 | Conditioning | The week's capacity task — ten minutes at most, scaled by whoever is present. |
| 52–62 | **Breath practice** | The named breathing exercise, taught as its own segment. |
| 62–72 | Verse circle | The deepening passage read twice. One question. Silence. One sentence each from anyone who wants to speak. |
| 72–75 | Close | Next week named. Anyone who wants to stay and talk is welcome to stay and talk. |

That is seventy-five minutes. In heat, cut to fifty by shortening the main work and the conditioning. For a solo athlete, keep the timer and the segments. In a crushing week, shorten the workout, never the verse circle.

## 5. The four-week rhythm inside every block

The year is thirteen blocks of four weeks. Each block has one theme, and inside each block the weeks follow the same wave — the oldest shape in endurance training, and the shape of the Christian life besides: stress, then rest, then growth.

**Week one — Base (teach).** The block's opening questions, held at conversational intensity, no hurry, a great deal of talk. The goal is that everyone leaves able to *name the week's concept* and say what it means for their own training. If people are exhausted, the pace was too hot. If people cannot state the concept, the teaching was too short.

**Week two — Build (repetition).** The block's questions returned to daily — morning check-in and evening review — quietly, without novelty. Volume without intensity. Correction (of form, of a misread zone) is offered quietly and individually, never as a public demonstration of someone's error.

**Week three — Peak (capacity).** The block's hardest question, held under genuine but controlled intensity — the interval week. This is the week that trains the emotional dimension, because effort is what makes people revert to their habits. Nobody is pushed past what they can recover from by the next morning. No all-nighters, no heroic stupidity.

**Week four — Recover (flow).** Light, integrated movement that uses the block's theme without demanding anything new — easy pace, good company — followed by a longer verse circle. The bring-a-friend week: arrive late, leave early, miss half, nothing is disrupted.

## 6. Why the breath practice sits where it does

The breath comes *after* the physical work and *before* the verse circle, on purpose. After forty-five minutes of movement, most people are mildly aroused: heart up, breathing fast, attention narrowed. A passage read in that state lands on a nervous system that cannot receive it. Eight to ten minutes of slow breathing with a long exhale brings the heart rate down and makes the room quiet enough that a single verse can actually be heard instead of merely recited.

This is also where the curriculum trains what most cardio culture trains badly: **calm, deliberate attention to the body's own signals.** Most fitness content produces a person who scans for burnable suffering and hoards suffering racked up. What we want instead is a person whose breath is nasal at easy effort, whose exhale is long under stress, who can read a heart-rate number without either panic or bravado. That is a trainable skill, practiced fifty-two times a year. Each breath practice has three jobs, named aloud when introduced:

1. **Down-regulate.** Long exhale, slow count, no strain. The parasympathetic job — where recovery actually happens.
2. **Widen.** Nasal, low, quiet breathing at easy effort — the aerobic-base job, the "run like a snail" job.
3. **Attend.** One verse, one number, one heartbeat, held before God. The stewardship job.

## Block 1 · Weeks 1–4 · The Core Principle: The Heart as Physical and Spiritual Center

### Week 1 · The First Organ to Work, the Last to Rest
**Focus work — base week.** The core principle, held at conversational pace: the heart is the physical and spiritual center, and this framework is about your evolving relationship with cardio across your whole life — identifying enjoyable activities rather than mere obligations. Walk or ski easy for the whole session and talk the whole time; the talk test *is* the workout.
**Breath practice — "The Hundred Thousand."** Sit still. Breathe in for four, out for six. Count your breaths for five minutes, then multiply: your heart did roughly ten thousand beats while you sat there, without asking you once.
**Concept.** The heart is the only muscle that cannot take a rest day, which is exactly why it deserves your training days.
**Deepening.** *Proverbs 4:23 — "Above all else, guard your heart, for everything you do flows from it."* And the material: your heart began beating before your mother knew you existed — the first organ to function, around three weeks after conception — and it will not stop until you do. Two percent of your body weight, twenty percent of your effort's worth: the Bible's "heart" and the one in your chest share the address.
**Teaching.** The framework's first move is a relationship, not a regimen: cardio is something you get to have a *relationship with*, across life stages, which means the questions matter more than the splits. Guard your heart, says the proverb — and this year we obey it twice, once in the prayer closet and once in Zone 2. Start where you actually are, not where you tell people you are.

### Week 2 · Obligation to Privilege
**Focus work — build week.** Daily, morning and evening: **Ready to stop treating cardio like punishment and start seeing it as privilege?** (*Psalm 118:24 — "The Lord has done it this very day; let us rejoice today and be glad."*) **Ready to treat your cardiovascular system like the life-sustaining miracle it is?** (*Leviticus 17:11 — "For the life of a creature is in the blood."*)
**Breath practice — "This Very Day."** In for four on the morning's complaint; out for six on the day that was given anyway. Eight rounds before the alarm is answered.
**Concept.** Punishment is assigned; privilege is chosen. Nobody dies with regret about having moved.
**Deepening.** *1 Corinthians 9:26 — "Therefore I do not run like someone running aimlessly."* And the material: the cultures with the longest-lived hearts — Okinawa, Sardinia, the Greek islands — never "work out"; they *move*, daily, for a lifetime. The difference between obligation and privilege is mostly the story you tell, and the story is yours to rewrite this week.
**Teaching.** The Ancient Guy energy in these questions is deliberately loud, but notice what it's loud *about*: not suffering, but gratitude — you aren't ready to die YET, which means today's capacity is a gift with a shelf life. Leviticus is right as physiology and as theology: the life is in the blood. Train like someone who got to keep it.

### Week 3 · The Norwegian Understanding: Snails, Deer, and the Brain's Oxygen Bill
**Focus work — base week.** The block's deepening question, taken slowly: **Why do the world's best endurance athletes — and the busiest CEOs — spend ninety percent of their training moving like snails?** Hold it for the whole hour, at the slowest pace you can sustain while talking.
**Breath practice — "Nasal Snail."** Nose only, in for four, out for six, on an easy walk. If you must open your mouth, you are going too fast — the breath sets the pace, not the other way around.
**Concept.** To run like a deer, spend ninety percent of your time running like a snail.
**Deepening.** *Isaiah 40:31 — "But those who hope in the Lord will renew their strength. They will soar on wings like eagles; they will run and not grow weary."* And the material: Alex Karp, who runs Palantir, cross-country skis five-plus hours a week near the White Mountains, mostly at the slowest pace a human can sustain — a method he credits to Norwegian skiers and to his former Norwegian commando bodyguards. "To run like a deer, you have to spend 90% of your time running like a snail," he told Axios in 2023 — with speed intervals once, maybe twice, a week. His VO2 max sits near 65, elite territory for his age, and he dates the real gains to 18 months, then 36. Why so slow, so long? Partly the mitochondria and the stroke volume; but Karp talks most about the *head*: the brain is two percent of your body mass and burns roughly twenty percent of your oxygen, and the Norwegians — who ski through dark winters at a snail's pace — understand that a well-oxygenated brain is the point of the whole project. The aerobic base you build in the legs pays its first dividends in the mind that skis through winters.
**Teaching.** This week is the curriculum's thesis in one image: the Norwegian method is patience formalized — an enormous base built slowly so that rare moments of speed can be devastating, and a brain kept so well-fed that thirty-six-month patience becomes possible. Notice the Christian shape of it: hope renews strength; the eagle soars because it waited on the updraft. Ancient Guy energy gets you out the door; the snail keeps you alive and thinking for decades.

### Week 4 · Stewardship: The Temple Has a Heart
**Focus work — recover week.** Light movement, longer verse circle, bring-a-friend week. The block's questions in a sentence each, then: **What does it mean that the body is a temple and not a warehouse?** — the stewardship question under everything this year.
**Breath practice — "Temple Breathing."** Stand easy. In for four, out for six, feeling the ribs open like doors. Five rounds, then one minute of stillness.
**Concept.** Stewardship is managing property that was never yours; the beats were leased, not owned.
**Deepening.** *1 Corinthians 6:19–20 — "Do you not know that your bodies are temples of the Holy Spirit? … Therefore honor God with your bodies."* And the material: cardiovascular fitness is the single strongest modifiable predictor of how long *and how well* you live — fitness, not thinness; capacity, not appearance. You cannot steward what you never measure, and you cannot measure what you never test. The year ahead is one long honest measurement.
**Teaching.** The block closes by fusing its two hearts: the one beating a hundred thousand times today, and the one Solomon said to guard above all else. A temple is a place where something dwells — so the stewardship question is not "am I sculpted?" but "is this house fit for its Guest and its work?" Next block: the daily questions, the small interrogations that make or break the base.

## Block 2 · Weeks 5–8 · Daily Cardiovascular Contemplation (Part I)

### Week 5 · Morning Heart Check-In
**Focus work — base week.** Retained, morning questions: **So that resting heart rate of yours — is it bragging about your fitness or tattling on your couch addiction?** (*Proverbs 4:23 — "Above all else, guard your heart."*) And: **Is your cardiovascular fitness ready for whatever life throws at you, or are you hoping for the best?** (*Luke 12:40 — "You also must be ready, because the Son of Man will come at an hour when you do not expect him."*)
**Breath practice — "The Morning Reading."** Before rising: pulse taken at the wrist, sixty seconds, written down. Then in for four, out for six, three rounds. The number is the lesson; the breath is the prayer.
**Concept.** Your resting heart rate is the cheapest lab test you own, and it files a report every single morning.
**Deepening.** *Psalm 139:23–24 — "Search me, God, and know my heart… see if there is any offensive way in me."* And the material: untrained adults rest around 60–100 beats per minute; endurance-trained hearts idle in the 40s, because a bigger stroke volume needs fewer beats. A morning reading taken for two weeks tells you your trend — and trend beats any single number, in cardiology and in sanctification alike.
**Teaching.** The framework's morning interrogation is really psalm-prayer with a strap: search me, O God, and also search my Fitbit. Take the reading before the coffee, before the news, and write it down — the unexamined heart, physical or spiritual, drifts. Readiness is the point of both questions: Luke's verse is about being ready at an hour you don't expect, and so is a resting heart rate of 52.

### Week 6 · The Miracle and the Punishment Reframe
**Focus work — build week.** Daily, retained: **Ready to treat your cardiovascular system like the life-sustaining miracle it is?** (*Leviticus 17:11 — "For the life of a creature is in the blood."*) And: **Ready to stop treating cardio like punishment and start seeing it as privilege?** (*Psalm 118:24 — "The Lord has done it this very day; let us rejoice today and be glad."*)
**Breath practice — "Blood and Breath."** In for four — the red cells loading; hold two — the exchange in the lungs; out for six — the delivery. Six rounds, feeling the whole circuit.
**Concept.** Every breath is a delivery of life to five liters of river, and the river reaches every cell you own.
**Deepening.** *Acts 17:25 — "He himself gives everyone life and breath and everything else."* And the material: your blood travels roughly 20,000 kilometers a day through vessels laid end to end twice around the planet — most of it through capillaries thinner than a hair, in a system that can reroute itself in seconds when a muscle starts asking for oxygen. Punishment does not have this level of engineering. Gift does.
**Teaching.** Hold the reframe for seven straight mornings, because the punishment story is old and stubborn: cardio-as-penance comes from a culture that treats bodies as sins needing payment. Paul told the Athenians God gives breath to everyone — your pulse is a gift with no invoice attached. Move today like someone unwrapping something, and let the psalm's logic run: this very day, therefore this very walk.

### Week 7 · Evening Recovery Assessment
**Focus work — base week.** Retained, evening questions: **How's that heart rate recovery — bouncing back like a champion or gasping like a fish?** (*Psalm 23:3 — "He refreshes my soul. He guides me along the right paths."*) And: **How's that sleep affecting your cardiovascular recovery — healing or hindering?** (*Psalm 127:2 — "In vain you rise early and stay up late… for he grants sleep to those he loves."*)
**Breath practice — "The Bounce."** Right after the day's last effort: note your heart rate, then breathe a long exhale for one minute, and note it again. The drop you see is your parasympathetic system doing its evening work.
**Concept.** The speed of the bounce tells you more than the height of the effort.
**Deepening.** *Mark 6:31 — "Come with me by yourselves to a quiet place and get some rest."* And the material: a healthy heart drops well over ten beats in the first minute after stopping; elite recoveries fall thirty and more. Sleep is when the stroke volume's dividends are banked — deep sleep is when heart rate and blood pressure reach their daily lows, and skimping it taxes tomorrow's session before it happens.
**Teaching.** The evening questions are the framework's honesty: the workout doesn't make you fitter — the *recovery from* the workout does, and the bounce is the receipt. Jesus scheduled rest for his own disciples with the same deliberateness he scheduled teaching, so take the assessment without guilt and without grading. A slow bounce or a short night is information for tomorrow, not a verdict on your worth.

### Week 8 · The Morning HRV Signal
**Focus work — recover week.** Retained: **Is that morning HRV telling you to charge ahead or pump the brakes?** (*Psalm 46:10 — "Be still, and know that I am God."*) The block's questions in a sentence each; longer verse circle.
**Breath practice — "Be Still."** Resonance breathing: in for five, out for five — six breaths a minute, the rate where heart and breath entrain. Five minutes, eyes closed.
**Concept.** HRV is the gas gauge; the wise driver reads it before the trip, not after the crash.
**Deepening.** *Ecclesiastes 3:1 — "There is a time for everything, and a season for every activity under the heavens."* And the material: heart rate variability — the tiny beat-to-beat differences — is your autonomic nervous system's daily memo: high and stable means recovered; suppressed and jittery means stress, illness, or overreaching. The skill is not worship of the number but response to it: a low morning says today is a snail day, and knowing that is what separates decades of training from seasons of injury.
**Teaching.** The block ends with the most modern question in the framework and the oldest answer in the Psalter: be still, and know — read the signal, then believe it. Charging ahead on a red gauge is not toughness; it is theft from next week's version of you. Next block: the weekly rhythm, Monday's interrogation through Sunday's yoke.

## Block 3 · Weeks 9–12 · The Weekly Training Rhythm (Part II)

### Week 9 · Monday: Foundation Assessment
**Focus work — base week.** Retained, Monday's questions: **When exactly were you planning to stop treating your cardiovascular system like an afterthought?** (*1 Corinthians 6:19–20 — "…your bodies are temples of the Holy Spirit."*) **Those 150 minutes of weekly cardio — are you crushing them or still negotiating with yourself?** (*Ecclesiastes 9:10 — "Whatever your hand finds to do, do it with all your might."*) **Ready to stop negotiating with your alarm clock about morning cardio?** (*Proverbs 6:9 — "How long will you lie there, you sluggard?"*) **How much longer will you wait to give your heart the training it deserves?** (*2 Corinthians 6:2 — "Now is the time of God's favor, now is the day of salvation."*)
**Breath practice — "Monday's Answer."** In for four on the question; out for six on the only answer that counts — shoes on. Six rounds, then go.
**Concept.** Monday exists to convert guilt into a plan; the plan is the point, the guilt is just fuel.
**Deepening.** *Lamentations 3:22–23 — "His compassions never fail. They are new every morning."* And the material: 150 minutes of moderate aerobic work a week is the floor every major health organization agrees on — roughly twenty minutes of honest movement a day, a bar so low that negotiating with it is the actual tell. And the negotiation has a market rate: habit research says the alarm-clock argument is lost or won in the first ninety seconds after waking.
**Teaching.** Monday's four questions are the framework opening with both barrels, and notice they are all the same question: *when?* — to which Solomon's sluggard, Paul's "now," and your resting heart rate all give the same answer. The compassion text is deliberate: new every morning means Monday is not a judgment, it's a mercy. Write the week's 150 minutes into a calendar today, because a plan in the head is a negotiation, and a plan on the calendar is a covenant.

### Week 10 · Tuesday: Zone Training — Ninety Percent Snail
**Focus work — build week.** Retained, Tuesday's questions: **That Zone 2 training everyone talks about — still pretending you don't know what it means?** (*Proverbs 4:7 — "The beginning of wisdom is this: Get wisdom."*) **Your heart rate zones — precisely calibrated or just winging it with '220 minus age'?** (*Proverbs 27:23 — "Be sure you know the condition of your flocks."*) **How many more articles about Zone 2 before you actually stay in Zone 2?** (*Proverbs 14:23 — "All hard work brings a profit, but mere talk leads only to poverty."*) **Those Zone 5 efforts — embracing the burn or running from the fire?** (*Daniel 3:17 — "The God we serve is able to deliver us from it."*)
**Breath practice — "The Zone 2 Check."** Nasal breathing only for the whole easy session; if you cannot keep your mouth closed and tell a story, you have left Zone 2. The nose is the gauge.
**Concept.** Zone 2 is the pace at which you can talk, nose closed, for an hour — and it is where the whole base is built.
**Deepening.** *Proverbs 4:26 — "Give careful thought to the paths for your feet; be steadfast in all your ways."* And the material: Zone 2 is roughly 60–75% of max heart rate — the intensity where fat oxidation runs high, lactate stays low, and mitochondria multiply; the Norwegians and Karp's commandos live here nine days out of ten. "220 minus age" is a 1970s estimate with error bars wide enough to drive a sled through; the talk test, a lab test, or a field test beat it. Zone 5 — the fire — is real and rare: short, hard intervals, one or two a week, embraced like Shadrach's crew embraced the furnace: with company and confidence.
**Teaching.** The most-quoted and least-practiced number in fitness is Zone 2, and the framework's Tuesday is designed to end the pretending: knowing the definition is nothing; staying in it while your ego begs for speed is the entire skill. Notice the verses shepherd you toward *condition-of-flocks* knowledge — your zones are your flock, and you count them weekly. And the discipline is remarkably Christian: the slow faithful plodding that builds cathedrals also builds capillaries.

### Week 11 · Wednesday Intervals, Thursday Metabolism
**Focus work — peak week.** Retained, Wednesday's questions: **Those interval sessions you're avoiding — they're starting to take it personally!** (*Hebrews 12:1 — "Let us run with perseverance the race marked out for us."*) **When will your actual training match your theoretical knowledge about HIIT?** (*James 1:22 — "Do not merely listen to the word… Do what it says."*) **Those tempo runs you keep postponing — they're starting to feel rejected!** (*Romans 12:11 — "Never be lacking in zeal."*) **Those Norwegian threshold sessions — too scary or just right for your ego to handle?** (*2 Timothy 1:7 — "The Spirit God gave us… power, love and self-discipline."*) And Thursday's: **That continuous glucose monitor data — is it applauding your metabolic flexibility or staging an intervention?** (*1 Corinthians 10:31 — "…do it all for the glory of God."*) **Your metabolic flexibility — switching fuels like a hybrid or stuck in one gear?** (*2 Corinthians 12:9 — "My grace is sufficient for you."*) **That fat oxidation rate — burning efficiently or dependent on constant sugar hits?** (*Matthew 4:4 — "Man shall not live on bread alone."*) **Your FATmax training — dialed in or still guessing at intensities?** (*Proverbs 24:27 — "Put your outdoor work in order… after that, build your house."*)
**Breath practice — "Interval Recovery."** After each hard rep: nose only, long exhales, until the breath is conversational again — the recovery breath is part of the rep, not a break from it.
**Concept.** Intervals are the two percent of the week that make the other ninety-eight percent count; metabolism is the fireplace the intervals feed.
**Deepening.** *Hebrews 12:11 — "No discipline seems pleasant at the time, but painful. Later on, however, it yields a peaceful harvest of righteousness."* And the material: the famous "Norwegian threshold session" is something like four eight-minute reps just below lactate threshold, with easy recovery between — the intensity where talk becomes broken sentences; the Norwegians used it to raise threshold while keeping their athletes healthy. Lactate itself is not the enemy but a fuel — shuttled around and burned by heart and muscle — and fat oxidation, trained by long Zone 2, is the hybrid gear: the better your fat-burning at easy pace, the more carbohydrate you keep for the moments that matter.
**Teaching.** The peak week holds the framework's two honest days at once: Wednesday's intervals, which everyone knows about and few schedule, and Thursday's metabolism, which everyone eats three times a day and few measure. James's verse is written for exactly this: hearing the HIIT sermon and doing the HIIT work are different sports. Take one interval session and one honest look at your fueling this week — and notice that the peace comes after the discipline, never before it.

### Week 12 · Friday Recovery, Saturday Variety, Sunday Rest
**Focus work — recover week.** Retained, Friday's questions: **Ready to admit your recovery protocols need as much attention as your workout plans?** (*Mark 6:31 — "Come with me… and get some rest."*) **Those recovery runs — actually recovering or just adding more fatigue?** (*Exodus 33:14 — "My Presence will go with you, and I will give you rest."*) **Your exercise-induced adaptations — maximizing or minimizing them with poor recovery?** (*Galatians 6:9 — "Let us not become weary in doing good, for at the proper time we will reap."*) **Your inflammatory markers — keeping them in check or fueling the fire with poor recovery?** (*Proverbs 17:14 — "Starting a quarrel is like breaching a dam."*) Saturday's: **That cross-training you're ignoring — it misses you, and your overused muscles need it!** (*1 Corinthians 12:21 — "The eye cannot say to the hand, 'I don't need you!'"*) **Ready to stop pretending that walking the dog counts as vigorous cardio?** (*1 Timothy 4:8 — "For physical training is of some value."*) **Those hill repeats calling your name — answering or sending to voicemail?** (*Psalm 24:3 — "Who may ascend the mountain of the Lord?"*) Sunday's: **Those easy days — actually easy or secretly racing yourself again?** (*Matthew 11:30 — "My yoke is easy and my burden is light."*) **Ready to graduate from the "all or nothing" cardio mentality?** (*Ecclesiastes 7:18 — "Whoever fears God will avoid all extremes."*) **Your training intensity distribution — actually distributed or always pushing hard?** (*Ecclesiastes 7:16 — "Do not be overrighteous… why destroy yourself?"*)
**Breath practice — "Sabbath Cadence."** For the whole easy session: in for four steps, out for four steps, walking pace. The breath sets a pace no ego can race.
**Concept.** Easy must be easy enough to be boring — the week's back half is where the gains are banked.
**Deepening.** *Isaiah 30:15 — "In repentance and rest is your salvation, in quietness and trust is your strength."* And the material: elite endurance athletes across every sport settle on roughly the same distribution — about 80% of volume easy, 20% hard, the polarized model Stephen Seiler documented in Norwegian skiers. The amateur's signature is the opposite: everything medium-hard, nothing truly easy, nothing truly hard, and the "recovery run" that becomes a slow race is the most common self-inflicted wound in the sport. Recovery is when the body rebuilds; stress is only the request.
**Teaching.** The block's final three days form the curriculum's quietest and most radical teaching: rest is a discipline, distribution is a virtue, and the amateur's extremes — all or nothing — are the same sin wearing gym clothes. Solomon and the sports scientists converge: avoid all extremes, distribute your intensity, and let the easy yoke actually be easy. Bring a friend on the Saturday session and let them set the pace; if you secretly race them, you have found your homework.

## Block 4 · Weeks 13–16 · Baseline and Heart Rate Mastery (Months 1–2)

### Week 13 · Honest Evaluation
**Focus work — base week.** Retained, Month 1: **When did walking up stairs become an Olympic event for your heart?** (*Psalm 121:1–2 — "I lift up my eyes to the mountains—where does my help come from?"*) **When did you last actually measure your fitness instead of assuming it's "pretty good"?** (*2 Corinthians 13:5 — "Examine yourselves… test yourselves."*) **Your cardiovascular age versus chronological age — winning or losing that race?** (*Psalm 103:5 — "…your youth is renewed like the eagle's."*)
**Breath practice — "The Stair Test."** Climb your usual flight at your usual pace; at the top, breathe only through the nose for one minute. Whatever that costs you is your baseline, and baselines are gifts.
**Concept.** You cannot progress what you refuse to measure; "pretty good" is not a number.
**Deepening.** *Proverbs 27:23 — "Be sure you know the condition of your flocks."* And the material: the field tests are free — a twelve-minute max-distance test (Cooper's) estimates VO2 max; a timed mile, a resting pulse trend, and a morning HRV together sketch the whole flock. Karp's VO2 max near 65 is boast-worthy precisely because it is *measured*; your unmeasured "pretty good" is a rumor you started about yourself.
**Teaching.** Month 1 of the framework is not training, it is inventory — the stairs, the watch, the mirror, all asked the same thing Paul asked Corinth: examine yourselves. The discomfort of honest measurement is the point: grace works on truth, and so does training. ### Week 15 · Understanding Your Heart
**Focus work — base week.** Retained, Month 2: **Your heart rate variability called — it says you're stressed. ### Week 16 · Heart Rate Application
**Focus work — build week.** Retained: **How accurately can you predict your heart rate for any given pace?** (*Proverbs 16:9 — "In their hearts humans plan their course, but the Lord establishes their steps."*) And: **That heart rate reserve — using it wisely or squandering it on junk miles?** (*Proverbs 31:16 — "She considers a field and buys it; out of her earnings she plants a vineyard."*)

**Deepening.** *1 Corinthians 9:25 — "Everyone who competes in the games goes into strict training."* And the material: heart rate reserve — the gap between your resting and maximum heart rates — is the honest denominator for training zones (the Karvonen method: target = resting + fraction × reserve), and it makes "junk miles" visible: medium-hard effort that builds neither base nor threshold, spent from a reserve that the Proverbs 31 woman would have invested. ## Block 5 · Weeks 17–20 · Threshold and Cardiac Adaptations (Months 3–4)

### Week 17 · Understanding Thresholds
**Focus work — base week.** Retained, Month 3: **Ready to stop letting your lactate threshold boss you around?** (*Philippians 4:13 — "I can do all this through him who gives me strength."*) And: **That ventilatory threshold — pushing it higher or letting it slide with age?** (*Isaiah 40:29 — "He gives strength to the weary."*)

**Concept.** Threshold is the fastest pace your breath can still pay for; it is trainable, and age has not repealed the policy.
**Deepening.** *Habakkuk 2:3 — "For the revelation awaits an appointed time; it speaks of the end and will not prove false."* And the material: your lactate threshold sits where lactate production begins outrunning clearance — roughly the hardest pace sustainable for about an hour — and it responds to training more reliably than VO2 max does; the ventilatory threshold is its cheap cousin, readable from your own breathing on any road. Raising it is the quiet project of every successful endurance decade, and it yields most to the boring base plus modest doses of Tuesday's threshold work.
**Teaching.** The framework gives threshold the strongest strength verse in the epistles, because threshold work is where you find out what "all things through Christ" means at 6 a.m. in February. ### Week 18 · Threshold Training — the Norwegian 4×8
**Focus work — peak week.** Retained: **How precisely do you know your lactate threshold versus how precisely you're guessing?** (*Proverbs 18:13 — "To answer before listening—that is folly and shame."*) And: **Ready to make friends with lactate instead of treating it like the enemy?** (*Matthew 5:44 — "Love your enemies."*)
**Breath practice — "Eight On, Two Off."** The session's own shape: eight minutes of threshold breathing (hard but rhythmic, nose optional), two minutes of long slow exhales. Four rounds. The recovery breath is written into the prescription.
**Concept.** Lactate is not the enemy; it is fuel with a bad reputation — and the Norwegian 4×8 is how you teach your body to burn it in friendship.
**Deepening.** *Romans 8:28 — "And we know that in all things God works for the good of those who love him."* And the material: physiologist George Brooks showed lactate is shuttled to heart and muscle and *burned as fuel* — the burn you fear is largely the shuttle system overflowing, and the 4×8-minute threshold intervals (just below threshold, short recoveries) that the Norwegian skiers popularized are precisely a lesson in staying just under the flood line. Karp's commandos would recognize the session; so would Paul, who saw the things that oppose you get recruited to serve you.
**Teaching.** This is the peak week: measure the line, then spend eight minutes at a time just under it, and notice — as promised — that the enemy has become a coworker. The spiritual rhyme is exact: the "enemy" was slandered by those who never listened to it. Love your enemies, says the Lord; buffer your lactate, say the Norwegians. Both commands mean: get close enough to understand what it's for.

### Week 19 · Cardiac Adaptations
**Focus work — build week.** Retained, Month 4: **How's that stroke volume — pumping like a fire hose or dripping like a leaky faucet?** (*Ezekiel 36:26 — "I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit in you."*) And: **Your cardiac output reserve — using it or losing it?** (*Matthew 25:14–30 — "Whoever has will be given more."*)
**Breath practice — "The Fire Hose."** Long, full breaths: in for six, out for six, feeling the complete fill and empty. Five minutes at rest — you are priming the pump you will call on later.
**Concept.** A trained heart moves more blood per beat, so it beats less to do the same work — grace economics in muscle tissue.
**Deepening.** *Psalm 93:4 — "Mightier than the thunder of the great waters… is the Lord on high."* And the material: stroke volume — blood per beat — grows with aerobic training and, thanks to Starling's law, responds most at high-end effort; the parable's "whoever has will be given more" is dry physiology: capacity used expands, capacity idle atrophies. The cardiac output reserve (heart rate × stroke volume) is your body's entire emergency budget — the stairs, the sprint for the bus, the decades.
**Teaching.** Month 4 opens with the framework's boldest metaphor: the trained heart as fire hose. Notice that the verse for it is Ezekiel's new heart — the physical pump and the recreated will were never separate in the framework's mind, and both conversions happen by grace *plus* cooperation. Use the reserve this week — one deliberately brisk session — because Jesus's economics and Starling's law agree on this: unused capacity is on loan and gets recalled.

### Week 20 · Eccentric Hypertrophy and the Long Game
**Focus work — recover week.** Retained: **That eccentric cardiac hypertrophy — earned through training or concerning your cardiologist?** (*Jeremiah 17:10 — "I the Lord search the heart."*) And: **Those cardiac adaptations — earning them through consistency or hoping for shortcuts?** (*Proverbs 13:11 — "Whoever gathers money little by little makes it grow."*)
**Breath practice — "Little by Little."** Slow, easy, nasal: in four, out six, for the whole of a gentle session. The count itself is the sermon.
**Concept.** The athlete's heart grows slowly, by remodeling from the inside — and the only shortcut is the one that ends in a cardiologist's office.
**Deepening.** *Isaiah 61:3 — "…that they may be called oaks of righteousness, a planting of the Lord for the display of his splendor."* And the material: endurance training grows the heart's chamber (eccentric hypertrophy) — a larger, slower, more efficient pump — while disease and steroid use grow its walls (concentric) — a stiffer, worse one; the difference is decades of little-by-little versus shortcuts. Jeremiah's searching of the heart applies to both kinds of growth: the body keeps honest books, and the imaging suite can tell earned remodeling from reckless.
**Teaching.** The block closes with the cardiologist's question and the sluggard's hope — because the fourth month of training is exactly when the shortcut ads start looking persuasive. The framework's answer is agrarian: gather little by little, oak by oak. Karp's thirty-six months, the Proverbs 13 investor, and the ecclesial year all run on the same clock. Train easy this week, consistently, and let the growth happen in the dark where all real remodeling happens.

## Block 6 · Weeks 21–24 · Vascular and Mitochondrial Foundations (Months 5–6)

It is a use-it-or-lose-it tissue, renewed by exactly the thing cardio supplies: steady flow.
**Teaching.** Month 5 goes interior — past the pump to the pipes — and finds the same gospel truth the psalmist found: stillness where there was storm. **Teaching.** The framework asks the birthday question because stiffness is silent: no symptoms until the wheel at the well groans. ### Week 34 · Peak Performance and the Oxygen Pulse
**Focus work — peak week.** Retained: **Your maximal oxygen pulse — optimized or operating at factory settings?** (*2 Peter 1:3 — "His divine power has given us everything we need."*) And: **Your chronotropic competence — heart rate responding appropriately or sluggish?** (*Ecclesiastes 3:11 — "He has made everything beautiful in its time."*)

**Concept.** Oxygen pulse is what each beat delivers; competence is rising when asked and settling when released — in hearts and in people.
**Deepening.** *Galatians 6:4–5 — "Each one should test their own actions… without comparing themselves with someone else."* And the material: oxygen pulse (O2 taken up per heartbeat) rises with training as stroke volume grows — the number cardiopulmonary labs use to spot a trained heart; and chronotropic competence — heart rate rising appropriately with load and recovering on schedule — is one of the strongest ordinary markers of a healthy autonomic system. **Teaching.** The peak week asks two precise questions of your pump's manners: does it deliver per beat, and does it rise and settle on time? **Teaching.** The summer questions are the same question wearing three hats — the framework really wants you to catch drift red-handed — because drift is where amateurs confuse fatigue with fitness and where honest long efforts get mismanaged into races. The key is consistency, wisdom, and gratitude for the capacity you have while working to maintain and improve it. As we strengthen the physical heart, we create capacity in the spiritual one; as we learn to endure physical challenges with grace, we develop spiritual endurance; as we learn to recover physically, we learn the spiritual discipline of rest.
